ABSTRACT
NutriGuard-AI  is  an  AI-assisted,  age-aware  framework  for  assessing  Indian 
packaged-food labels in a transparent and reproducible manner. The system  
combines  multimodal  label  interpretation  for  information  acquisition  with  a 
separate  deterministic  scoring  engine  that  evaluates  four  assessment  pillars: 
nutrition, ingredients, processing, and additive exposure. Age-specific reference  
configurations are used for child, teen, adult, and elderly profiles, and a NOVA-
based  processing  adjustment  is  incorporated  into  the  score.  The  application 
additionally  provides  evidence-oriented  explanations,  score  breakdowns,  
product comparisons, allergen information, and counterfactual what-if analysis.  
In  the  completed  Phase  I  implementation,  an  evaluation  collection  of  100  
structured  packaged-food  records  was  used  to  test  bounded  scoring,  
deterministic  repeatability,  component  ablation,  age  sensitivity,  coefficient  
perturbation, NOVA perturbation, missing-data handling, category sensitivity,  
counterfactual behavior, and external reference comparison. All 100 records  
passed  the  basic  score-bound,  grade-generation,  and  determinism  checks  
without crashes. The largest mean ablation effects were observed for nutrition  
(20.52 points) and NOVA (16.50 points), while age produced a mean effect of  
3.31 points. Controlled NOVA perturbation produced a mean score swing of  
35.58 points across the evaluated records. On 56 products for which Nutri-Score 
2023  classification  was  robust,  the  continuous  NutriGuard  score  showed  
Spearman correlation of 0.687 with inverse Nutri-Score class, while exact five-
class agreement was 28.6%. These results demonstrate the internal consistency  
and sensitivity of the implemented software framework; they do not establish  
clinical validity, health-outcome superiority, or population-level effects. Phase  
II will focus on further validation, refinement, and final project presentation.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 GENERAL
Packaged foods have become an important part of modern food consumption,  
and their labels provide structured information such as energy, macronutrients,  
ingredients,  additives,  and  other  nutritional  characteristics.  However,  
interpreting a food label requires users to consider several different types of  
information simultaneously, including nutrient quantities, serving information,  
ingredient composition, processing characteristics, and product classification.  
For many consumers, manually combining these heterogeneous factors can be  
difficult  and  time-consuming.  The  increasing  availability  of  artificial  
intelligence (AI), multimodal models, optical character recognition, retrieval-
augmented generation, and large language models has created opportunities for  
developing intelligent systems that can assist users in understanding food labels. 
NutriGuard-AI is developed as an intelligent information system for assessing  
Indian packaged foods in a transparent and structured manner. The system uses  
multimodal label processing to obtain a structured representation of a product  
and applies a deterministic scoring mechanism based on nutrition, ingredients,  
processing,  additives,  NOVA  classification,  and  age-sensitive  factors.  The  
numerical assessment is deliberately separated from the generative explanation  
layer  so  that  the  final  score  is  reproducible  and  its  component-level  
contributions can be inspected. 
1.2 NEED FOR THE STUDY

2
The major need for this study arises from the difficulty of converting the large  
amount  of  information  available  on  packaged-food  labels  into  an  
understandable and consistent assessment. Existing AI-based nutrition systems  
have  demonstrated  the  usefulness  of  multimodal  processing,  retrieval,  
knowledge  graphs,  and  language  models,  but  many  primarily  focus  on  
personalized  recommendations,  dietary  intervention,  or  natural-language  
interpretation. In such systems, users may receive a useful explanation without  
having clear visibility into how individual factors contribute to a numerical food  
assessment. Therefore, there is a need for a system in which the numerical  
reasoning process is explicit, decomposable, and reproducible rather than being  
dependent on generated language alone. 
The  study  therefore  focuses  on  developing  a  transparent  packaged-food  
assessment framework that can combine multiple label characteristics within a  
single  deterministic  mechanism.  In  addition  to  considering  nutritional  and  
ingredient information, the proposed system incorporates processing, additives,  
NOVA classification, and age-specific configurations for child, teen, adult, and  
elderly groups. This approach makes it possible to examine how changes in  
individual  factors  influence  the  computed  score  and  to  provide  controlled  
“what-if” scenarios through counterfactual analysis. The study is intended to  
establish the reproducibility and inspectability of the implemented system; it  
does not claim clinical validity, health-outcome prediction, or superiority over  
established nutritional scoring systems.
1.3 OBJECTIVES OF THE STUDY
The primary objective of the study is to develop an AI-assisted system capable  
of analysing Indian packaged-food labels and presenting their information in a  
structured  and  understandable  form.  The  system  aims  to  extract  relevant  
information  from  food  labels,  including  nutritional  quantities,  ingredients,  

3
additives,  processing  characteristics,  product  category,  and  NOVA  
classification. These extracted attributes are converted into a structured product  
representation that can be processed by a deterministic scoring engine. The  
system is designed to generate a bounded 0–100 assessment and expose the  
contribution of individual scoring components, thereby improving transparency  
and reproducibility. 
A further objective is to provide age-aware and explainable assessment rather  
than presenting only a single numerical value. The system supports child, teen,  
adult,  and  elderly  configurations  and  enables  comparison  of  scores  under  
different  age  parameters.  It  also  aims  to  generate  evidence-grounded  
explanations  separately  from  numerical  scoring  and  provide  algorithmic  
counterfactual  scenarios  that  demonstrate  how  selected  changes  to  product  
characteristics can affect the computed score. Finally, the study evaluates the  
implemented framework through deterministic verification, component ablation, 
age  sensitivity,  NOVA  perturbation,  counterfactual  analysis,  missing-data  
handling,  category  sensitivity,  and  statistical  analysis  over  the  available  
evaluation records.

CHAPTER 2
REVIEW OF LITERATURE
2.1 INTRODUCTION
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has increasingly been applied to food, nutrition, and  
healthcare  applications  to  support  information  analysis,  personalization,  and 
decision-making. Recent systems use technologies such as  Large Language  
Models (LLMs), multimodal AI, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG),  
knowledge  graphs,  and  recommendation  systems  to  process  nutritional  
information and generate user-oriented insights. These approaches demonstrate  
that AI can combine different sources of food and nutrition information and  
present the results in a more accessible form. 
However, nutrition-related AI systems differ considerably in their objectives  
and architectures. Some focus on personalized dietary recommendations, while  
others  concentrate  on  clinical  intervention,  food  recognition,  or  knowledge-
based  retrieval.  A  common  challenge  is  maintaining  transparency  in  the  
numerical  reasoning  process.  A  generated  recommendation  may  be  
understandable to the user while the contribution of individual nutritional or  
food-label factors remains difficult to determine. This creates a need for systems 
that combine AI-based information extraction and explanation with a clearly  
defined and reproducible numerical assessment mechanism.
2.2 EXISTING NUTRITION SCORING SYSTEMS
Existing  AI-based  nutrition  systems  generally  process  food  and  dietary  
information  using  multimodal  models,  recommendation  algorithms,  RAG  

5
pipelines, or knowledge graphs. These systems can integrate information such  
as food descriptions, ingredients, nutrient profiles, images, user preferences, and 
health-related requirements. For example, multimodal LLM-based systems can  
create unified representations of food items using textual, visual, ingredient, and  
nutritional information. 
Despite these developments, many existing systems primarily target  dietary 
recommendation or personalized nutrition guidance  rather than transparent  
assessment  of  individual  packaged-food  products.  The  numerical  decision  
process may also depend on complex models that make it difficult to identify  
the exact influence of individual factors. 
2.3 AI IN HEALTHCARE AND NUTRITION
AI is increasingly being explored in healthcare and nutrition for applications  
involving personalized recommendations, dietary planning, decision support,  
and health-related information processing. In nutrition, AI can combine large  
volumes  of  heterogeneous  information  and  assist  users  in  interpreting  
nutritional  data.  Recent  research  has  particularly  explored  LLMs  and  RAG 
systems because they can combine natural-language interaction with structured  
knowledge  sources.  Research  on  precision  nutrition  also  identifies  domain-
specific  adaptation,  knowledge  grounding,  interpretability,  and  reliable  
deployment as important challenges for AI-based nutrition systems. 
The use of AI in nutrition also introduces the requirement for  reliability and  
explainability.  LLM-based  systems  can  generate  fluent  responses  but  may  
suffer from hallucinations or insufficient grounding when factual information is  
required. Knowledge graphs and RAG can improve access to structured domain  
information,  while  deterministic  components  can  provide  more  predictable  
numerical behavior. Recent work therefore explores combinations of structured  

6
knowledge,  multimodal  information,  and  generative  models  to  improve  
nutrition-related decision support. NutriGuard-AI follows this general direction  
but restricts its numerical reasoning to an explicit deterministic scoring engine  
rather than allowing the generative model to act as the numerical authority.
2.4 LITERATURE SURVEY
2.4.1 Prasad et al. — 2026
A.  Prasad,  A.  S.,  B.  R.,  and  V.  G.  proposed  “NutritionWise:  Converting 
Nutritional  Labels  into  Personalized,  AI-Driven  Nutritional  Insights.”  The  
system  is  designed  to  convert  complex  nutritional-label  information  into  
personalized and understandable nutritional insights. It employs a multimodal  
image-and-text processing architecture, where nutritional labels are processed  
using  TrOCR  for  optical  character  recognition.  Large  language  models,  
including LLaMA-3.2-1B and LLaMA-3-8B, are used to generate explanations  
and  conversational  nutritional  support.  The  framework  further  incorporates  
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), FAISS, and SentenceTransformer for  
semantic  retrieval,  allergen  matching,  and  contextual  information  retrieval.  
Nutritional information is supported using sources such as OpenFoodFacts and  
WHO/FDA nutritional guidelines. The system also considers dietary suitability  
and personalized nutrition requirements based on individual goals.
2.4.2 Dindukurthi et al. — 2026
V.  Dindukurthi,  D.  Jain,  A.  Tripathi,  J.  M.  Obbineni,  and  I.  Kandasamy  
proposed “An Explainable Graph Retrieval Augmented Generation Framework  
for Personalized Nutrition Recommendation.” The framework employs a Graph  
Retrieval-Augmented  Generation  (GraphRAG)  architecture  with  a  Neo4j  
knowledge  graph  representing  relationships  among  diseases,  nutrients,  food  
items, symptoms, and demographic-specific Recommended Dietary Allowances  

7
(RDAs). A semantic ETL pipeline is used to integrate heterogeneous nutritional  
and  clinical  datasets  and  resolve  terminology  differences  using  embedding-
based alignment. During inference, the system retrieves nutritional requirements  
from  the  graph  and  ranks  candidate  foods  using  a  deterministic  cosine-
similarity-based ranking algorithm. The language model is restricted primarily  
to  formatting  graph-validated  outputs  rather  than  performing  unconstrained  
nutritional reasoning.
2.4.3 Research Gap Identified from the Literature
The  reviewed  literature  demonstrates  significant  progress  in  AI-based  
nutritional-label  interpretation,  multimodal  food  analysis,  personalized  
nutrition, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, knowledge graphs, and explainable  
recommendation  systems.  NutritionWise  demonstrates  the  effectiveness  of  
multimodal  label  processing  and  generative  AI  for  converting  nutritional  
information into personalized and user-friendly insights, while Dindukurthi et  
al. demonstrate how structured knowledge graphs and deterministic ranking can  
improve  explainability  and  demographic  sensitivity  in  nutrition  
recommendation.
However,  both  approaches  primarily  address  nutritional  interpretation  or  
personalized dietary recommendation rather than the problem of assigning a  
transparent  and  reproducible  quality  score  to  an  individual  packaged-food  
product. In particular, the reviewed works do not provide the same emphasis on  
a  deterministic,  decomposable  scoring  mechanism  specifically  designed  for  
Indian packaged foods, where multiple factors such as nutrient composition,  
ingredients,  additives,  processing  level,  product  category,  and  NOVA  
classification need to be considered together.

CHAPTER 3
METHODOLOGY AND SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
3.1 INTRODUCTION
NutriGuard-AI follows a structured methodology that converts packaged-food  
label information into a transparent and age-aware assessment. The system first  
extracts  and  organizes  label  information  such  as  nutrition,  ingredients,  
processing,  additives,  product  category,  and  NOVA  classification.  This  
structured  information  is  then  processed  by  a  deterministic  scoring  engine,  
while  the  explanation  component  uses  stored  evidence  to  provide  
understandable  results.  The  architecture  separates  numerical  scoring  from  
generative  explanation  so  that  the  final  score  remains  reproducible  and  
inspectable. 
3.2 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The  NutriGuard-AI  architecture  is  organized  into  two  main  layers :  the  
Intelligent Processing Layer  and the  Application and Presentation Layer . 
The  Intelligent  Processing  Layer  handles  label  extraction,  data  structuring,  
scoring,  and  age-based  assessment,  while  the  Application  and  Presentation  
Layer  provides  explanations,  recommendations,  comparisons,  and  
counterfactual insights to the user. This separation ensures that the generative  
AI component does not act as the source of numerical truth. 
3.3 LAYER 1 – INTELLIGENT PROCESSING LAYER
The Intelligent Processing Layer is responsible for converting the food label  
into structured information and calculating the food assessment. It receives label 

9
images or structured product data, extracts nutrition and ingredient information,  
identifies processing, additives, category, and NOVA classification, and applies  
the deterministic scoring function with the selected age group. The output is a  
reproducible score in the range of 0–100. 
3.4 LAYER 2 – APPLICATION AND PRESENTATION LAYER
The  Application  and  Presentation  Layer  converts  the  processed  results  into 
meaningful information for the user. It presents the final score, component-wise  
contributions, age-sensitive results, evidence-grounded explanations, ingredient  
and  allergen  information,  comparisons,  and  counterfactual  improvement  
suggestions.  The  explanation  is  generated  separately  from  the  numerical  
calculation and therefore does not modify the deterministic score. 
3.5 MODULE 1 – LABEL ANALYSIS MODULE
The Label Analysis Module accepts food-label images or structured information  
and identifies important product attributes. It extracts and organizes nutritional  
values, ingredients, additives, processing-related information, product category,  
and  NOVA  classification  into  a  structured  product  representation.  This  
structured representation acts as the input for the subsequent scoring process. 
3.6 MODULE 2 – DETERMINISTIC SCORING MODULE
The Deterministic Scoring Module calculates the food score using nutrition,  
ingredient, processing, additive, and NOVA factors. The scoring components  
are combined using predefined algorithmic parameters, followed by configured  
penalties and final score clipping to the 0–100 range. Since the calculation is  
deterministic,  the  same  structured  product  information  and  configuration  
produce the same numerical result. 
3.7 MODULE 3 – AGE AND PERSONALIZATION MODULE

10
The Age and Personalization Module adapts the assessment according to four  
age configurations:  child, teen, adult, and elderly . Age-sensitive nutritional  
thresholds  are  applied  while  keeping  the  underlying  product  representation  
unchanged. This allows the system to calculate and compare how the same  
packaged food is assessed for different age groups. 
3.8 MODULE 4 – EXPLANATION AND RECOMMENDATION 
MODULE
3.9 FOUR-PILLAR NUTRIGUARD SCORING FRAMEWORK
NutriGuard-AI  decomposes  the  assessment  into  four  deterministic  pillars:  
nutrition, ingredients, processing, and additive exposure. The implemented pre-
adjustment score is computed as S_pre = M_NOVA × (0.20I + 0.15P + 0.30A)  
+ 0.35N, followed by configured safeguards and clipping to the 0–100 range.  
The coefficients are fixed engineering parameters of the current prototype rather  
than values learned from health-outcome data.
3.10 AGE-AWARE NUTRIENT NORMALIZATION
For nutrient i and age group a, the normalized exposure is p(i,a) = (x_i / R_i,a)  
× 100, where x_i is the declared nutrient amount and R_i,a is the configured  
age-specific reference amount. Negative nutrient dimensions use exponential  
penalties,  while  positive  nutrient  contributions  use  saturating  functions.  
Representative  prototype  references  cover  total  sugar,  added  sugar,  sodium, 
saturated fat, trans fat, caffeine, and fiber for child, teen, adult, and elderly  
configurations.

3.11 NOVA PROCESSING ADJUSTMENT
The  prototype  applies  NOVA  multipliers  of  1.00,  0.90,  0.70,  and  0.50  for  
NOVA 1 through NOVA 4, with an additional pre-aggregation cap for NOVA-4 
cases.  NOVA  therefore  operates  as  a  high-leverage  processing  adjustment  
within the deterministic scoring pipeline.
3.12 SAFETY AND DATA-QUALITY SAFEGUARDS
The  implementation  includes  checks  for  undeclared  ingredients,  undeclared  
mandatory nutrients, undeclared allergens, and amplified-exposure categories.  
Missing  and  alias  nutrient  fields  are  normalized  before  scoring.  These  
safeguards are implementation controls and are not presented as clinical cutoffs  
or toxicological risk estimates.
3.13 COUNTERFACTUAL ANALYSIS
Counterfactual analysis modifies an eligible product attribute and recomputes  
the  score  using  ΔS  =  S(X′,a)  −  S(X,a).  The  mechanism  exposes  how  the  
implemented scoring function responds to controlled changes in attributes such  
as added sugar, saturated fat, sodium, total sugar, or calories. These scenarios  
describe  model-internal  behavior  and  are  not  causal  estimates  of  health  
outcomes.
The Explanation and Recommendation Module presents the calculated result in  
an understandable form using evidence-grounded information. It provides score  
explanations,  component  contributions,  comparisons,  and  algorithmic  
counterfactual suggestions showing how changes to selected product factors  

12
could  affect  the  score.  These  counterfactuals  are  treated  as  computational  
scenarios rather than predictions of real-world health outcomes.

IMPLEMENTATION AND PRELIMINARY RESULTS
4.1 DATASET AND TECHNOLOGY STACK
The  evaluation  dataset  consists  of  100  packaged-food  product  records  
maintained  within  the  project  dataset.  The  dataset  is  used  as  an  evaluation 
collection  and  is  not  considered  statistically  representative  of  the  complete

project  data,  ingredient  information,  additive  information,  and  evidence  
resources maintained as application data resources.
Internal  evaluation  was  performed  to  verify  the  consistency  and  behavioral 
properties of the implemented scoring system. The evaluation checked score  
bounds,  grade  generation,  deterministic  repeatability,  execution  success,  
component  ablation,  age  sensitivity,  NOVA  perturbation,  counterfactual  
behavior,  missing-data  handling,  category  sensitivity,  and  statistical  
comparisons. All 100 evaluated records passed the basic bounded-score, grade-
generation, and determinism checks with zero crashes.

representation, deterministic assessment, evidence/explanation, and application  
layers.  The  current  web  application  uses  React  and  TypeScript  with  Vite,

integration for multimodal label interpretation and explanation.

CONCLUSIONS AND WORK SCHEDULE
5.1 CONCLUSIONS
NutriGuard-AI provides an age-aware and explainable approach for assessing  
Indian packaged-food labels by combining multimodal information extraction  
with  a  deterministic  scoring  mechanism.  The  system  considers  nutrition,  
ingredients, processing, additives, NOVA classification, and age-related factors  
to  generate  a  transparent  0–100  assessment.  The  implemented  architecture  
separates numerical  scoring  from evidence-grounded  explanation,  improving  
reproducibility  and  allowing  individual  scoring  factors  to  be  examined  
independently..
The present evaluation is limited by the use of 100 product records and should  
not  be  interpreted  as  a  representative  survey  of  the  Indian  packaged-food  
market.  Age-specific  references,  coefficients,  thresholds,  and  penalties  are  
engineering parameters of the current prototype rather than clinically calibrated  
quantities.  NOVA  is  a  high-leverage  dependency  and  the  recorded  NOVA  
distribution is imbalanced. Upstream label-extraction errors can propagate to the 
deterministic  engine,  while  OCR  accuracy  has  not  yet  been  benchmarked  
quantitatively. Explanation quality has not been evaluated using standardized  
factuality  measures  or  expert-rating  protocols.  Internal  B1–B4  variants  are  
controlled  software  comparisons  rather  than  external  published  baselines.  
Counterfactual results characterize changes in the model’s own score and do not  
represent real-world health effects.

Abstract—Packaged-food labels contain nutritional, ingredient,
additive, allergen, and processing information as separate signals
that are difficult to integrate consistently. Existing AI nutrition
systems commonly emphasize extraction, recommendation, or
natural-language interpretation, while the numerical assessment
pathway can remain difficult to inspect and reproduce. This
paper presents NutriGuard-AI, an age-aware framework for
deterministic assessment of Indian packaged-food labels. Mul-
timodal label interpretation is used for information acquisition
and evidence-oriented explanation, while a separate deterministic
engine performs numerical assessment. The engine combines four
assessment pillars—nutrition, ingredients, processing, and addi-
tive exposure—with age-specific reference-intake normalization,
a NOV A-based processing adjustment, data-quality safeguards,
and counterfactual score analysis.
A reproducible evaluation was conducted on 100 structured
product records using software verification, component ablation,
age sensitivity, coefficient perturbation, controlled NOV A pertur-
bation, counterfactual analysis, missing-data handling, category
sensitivity, and paired statistical tests. All 100 records produced
bounded and deterministic scores. Removing nutrition and NOV A
components produced mean score changes of 20.52 and 16.50
points, respectively, while the age component produced a mean
change of 3.31 points. Coefficient perturbation of±50%pre-
served product-ranking correlations between 0.968 and 0.998.
Controlled NOV A perturbation produced a mean score swing
of 35.58 points and changed the grade of all records. On
56 products with robust Nutri-Score 2023 classification, the
continuous NutriGuard score correlated with inverse Nutri-
Score class atρ= 0.687(p= 4.86×10 −9), while exact
five-class agreement was 28.6% (κ= 0.193). These findings
characterize reproducibility, sensitivity, and internal consistency
of the implemented framework; they do not establish clinical
validity, health-outcome superiority, or population-level effects.

I. INTRODUCTION
Food labels provide consumers with structured information
about energy, macronutrients, sugars, sodium, fats, ingredients,
additives, allergens, serving sizes, and processing character-
istics. Interpreting these fields together is difficult because
information is distributed across tables and ingredient lists, and
the importance of an exposure depends partly on demographic
context. A useful computational assistant must therefore move
beyond isolated nutrient extraction and combine heterogeneous
signals into a single, interpretable, and reproducible assess-
ment.
Recent AI systems for nutrition combine multimodal
extraction, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), knowl-
edge graphs, and large language models (LLMs). Assiri et
al. demonstrated automated nutritional-information extraction
from bilingual food labels using GPT-4o, GPT-4V , and Gem-
ini [1]. Dindukurthi et al. proposed an explainable GraphRAG
framework with structured ranking beneath an LLM expla-
nation layer [2]. Hu et al. demonstrated language-model-
based NOV A classification from ingredient text [3]. Other
systems address clinical nutrition intervention, multimodal
recommendation, Healthy Eating Index-informed recommen-
dation, and multi-agent nutrition coaching [4], [6]–[8]. These
works establish useful individual capabilities, but they gen-
erally emphasize extraction, recommendation, intervention, or
processing classification rather than a decomposable numerical
score for a single packaged-food label.
Explainability is a second challenge. A generated recom-
mendation may be persuasive while providing limited visibil-
ity into how individual label attributes affected a numerical

II. RELATEDWORK
A. AI-Based Food-Label Extraction
AI-based food-label extraction has established that multi-
modal models can convert label images into structured nutri-
tional information, but extraction alone does not define a repro-
ducible scoring mechanism [1]. Assiri et al. investigated GPT-
4o, GPT-4V , and Gemini for extracting nutritional information
from bilingual Arabic–English labels using a manually curated
dataset of 294 labels [1]. Their results establish the feasibility
of automated extraction while showing that structured post-
processing is still required. NutriGuard-AI treats extraction as
an upstream information-acquisition stage rather than as the
final decision mechanism.
B. Personalized and Structured Nutrition Systems
Dindukurthi et al. proposed a graph-centric personalized-
nutrition framework combining structured graph retrieval and
deterministic ranking with an LLM explanation layer [2].
Wu et al. introduced NutriOrion, a hierarchical multi-agent
framework grounded in clinical guidelines for personalized
nutrition intervention, evaluated on a clinical cohort [4]. Prasad
et al. presented NutritionWise, combining OCR, retrieval, and
LLM-based nutritional insight generation for label interpreta-
tion [5]. Deng and Tu proposed a multimodal LLM framework
for nutrition-aware dietary recommendation [6], Wang et al.
investigated an LLM-RAG approach informed by the Healthy
Eating Index using NHANES/FPED data [7], and Shikara et
al. presented NutriCoach, a multi-agent RAG framework for
personalized recommendation [8].
These systems collectively demonstrate that multimodal
extraction, retrieval, and generative reasoning are useful for
nutrition applications, but their primary objectives are rec-
ommendation or intervention rather than a decomposable,
numerically transparent packaged-food score.
NutritionWise is particularly close in problem setting be-
cause it converts nutritional labels into personalized AI-driven
insights using multimodal processing, OCR, retrieval, and
LLMs [5]. NutriGuard-AI differs in emphasis: its central
object is a decomposable numerical assessment whose com-
putation is deterministic.
C. Food Processing and NOVA Classification
Hu et al. used a fine-tuned language model for automated
NOV A classification from ingredient-list text across several
national food databases [3], showing that processing level is a
learnable and operationally consequential signal. NutriGuard-
AI instead uses NOV A as one operational dimension within a
broader deterministic score. The distinction is therefore not
the use of AI for nutrition itself, but the combination of
label-oriented multimodal acquisition, age-aware normaliza-
tion, deterministic multi-factor scoring, perturbation analysis,
and separated evidence-oriented explanation.
Table I positions NutriGuard-AI against representative re-

B. Label Acquisition and Structured Representation
A multimodal language model interprets food-label images

scoring. This representation makes the downstream scoring
function independent of the wording of an explanation.

additional pre-aggregation cap for NOV A-4 cases.

mandatory nutrients, undeclared allergens, and amplified-
exposure categories. These safeguards are implementation
rules rather than clinical cutoffs.
E. Counterfactual Analysis
Counterfactual analysis modifies an eligible attribute to form
a modified inputX ′ and computes
∆S=S(X ′, a)−S(X, a).(14)
The purpose is to expose how the implemented scoring rules
respond to controlled changes such as reduced added sugar,
saturated fat, sodium, total sugar, or calories.
Counterfactuals describe model-internal behavior and are
not causal estimates of health outcomes.

IV. SYSTEMARCHITECTURE ANDIMPLEMENTATION
The architecture consists of five layers:
1) Acquisition
2) Structured data
3) Deterministic assessment
4) Evidence/explanation
5) Application
At runtime, the label image is interpreted into structured
fields; units, serving sizes, nutrient names, and missing-field
conditions are normalized; the selected age profile is ap-
plied; ingredient, processing, additive, and NOV A logic are
evaluated; the score is bounded and converted to a grade;
and the explanation is assembled from the already-computed
numerical result and available evidence.
The scoring engine is implemented as a separate computa-
tional module. Its configuration contains age-specific reference
values, nutrient penalty coefficients, category weights, ingre-
dient rules, additive mappings, NOV A multipliers, and grade
thresholds.
The application is a React/TypeScript web application using
Vite, Tailwind CSS, Zustand, and a client-side Gemini inte-
gration for extraction and explanation. Evaluation scripts for
ablation, age sensitivity, baseline variants, category analysis,
counterfactual analysis, NOV A sensitivity, scoring verification,
statistics, and figure generation were executed on September 4,
2026 using Node.js v22.17.0 without pseudorandom-number
generation.
V. EXPERIMENTALSETUP ANDRESULTS
The evaluation uses 100 structured packaged-food records
spanning beverages, chocolates, biscuits, snacks, dairy prod-
ucts, cereals, nuts, seeds, and protein-oriented products.
The collection is an evaluation set rather than a statisti-
cally representative survey of Indian packaged foods. All 100
records contained the required fields for score generation, with
zero engine missing-data flags.
The evaluation deliberately probes multiple properties rather
than relying on a single accuracy metric.
A. Software Verification and Ablation
Software verification checks score bounds, grade generation,
deterministic repeatability, and execution success. All 100
records passed these checks with zero crashes.
Component ablation disables one major factor at a time
while retaining the others. All tested ablation comparisons
were statistically significant using paired Wilcoxon signed-
rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction atα= 0.05.
Table III shows that nutrition and NOV A removal generated
the largest mean score changes, followed by additives, ingre-
dients, and age. Processing produced a much smaller mean
change in this particular evaluation set.
Nutrition ablation produced a mean paired difference of
20.52 points with Cohen’sd= 2.746, while NOV A ablation
produced 16.50 points withd= 2.012. These statistics
indicate sensitivity of the implemented score to configured
components; they do not establish external validity.

B. Age Sensitivity
Age sensitivity compares the same product representation
under different age configurations.
As shown in Table IV, adult–child produced a mean differ-

These results demonstrate that the age parameter can alter
the computed assessment while the product input remains
fixed.
C. Parameter Robustness
Coefficient perturbation varied principal component weights
by−50%to+50%. The perturbation preserved the unit-sum
constraint by proportionally redistributing the induced weight
difference among the remaining coefficients.
Across all tested perturbations, maximum absolute score
change was at most 7 points, rank correlations remained above
0.967, and the consolidated analysis reports a range of 0.968–
0.998.
Grade changes ranged from 5% to 26%, depending on
component and perturbation magnitude.
These results indicate relatively stable product ordering un-
der the tested perturbations, while discrete grades can change
near grade boundaries. They do not demonstrate that the
selected coefficients are optimal or clinically calibrated.
D. NOVA Perturbation and Reference Alignment
Controlled perturbation of NOV A classification produced a
mean score swing of 35.58 points (median 35.00, SD 5.90,
range 22–53), with a grade change in all 100 evaluated records.
This confirms that the NOV A multiplier is operationally
influential in the current formulation and that upstream NOV A
misclassification could propagate substantially into the final
score.

by the scoring pipeline, the NOV A analysis is treated as

E. Counterfactual Analysis
Counterfactual modification was applicable to all 100
records, of which 53 yielded a successfully processed coun-
terfactual and 47 had no applicable modification case.
Among processed cases, score improvements ranged from
1 to 9 points (mean 3.36, median 3.00, SD 1.95), and all 53
cases exhibited a grade transition.
Dominant modification factors were primarily added sugar
and saturated fat, followed by total sugar, sodium, and calories.
Representative examples include:
•A score changing from 23 to 30 after an added-sugar
reduction.
•A score changing from 55 to 60 after a saturated-fat
reduction for Makino Roasted Masala Cashews.
•A score changing from 63 to 70 after a sodium reduction.
These results illustrate configuration-internal “what-if” be-
havior and are not causal estimates of real-world health
outcomes.
F . Missing Data, Category Sensitivity, and Baseline Variants
No evaluated record contained missing required fields; the
engine’s missing-data checks correctly produced zero flags
and valid numeric outputs for all 100 records, demonstrating
implementation handling rather than robustness to severe real-
world label incompleteness.
Category sensitivity produced a mean score swing of 0.67
points (median 1) with 10 grade changes, indicating that
category-dependent logic is present but less influential than
NOV A perturbation under the tested conditions.
Four nested internal configurations were evaluated:
•B1: nutrition only
•B2: nutrition + ingredients
•B3: nutrition + ingredients + processing
•B4: full adult configuration
These are controlled internal variants, not external state-of-
the-art baselines.
G. External Reference Comparison: Nutri-Score 2023
For external reference comparison, the 100-product col-
lection was evaluated against the updated Nutri-Score 2023
framework [9].
Because fruit, vegetable, and legume percentages were un-
available for many composite products, FVL-dependent cases
were not assigned assumed values.
Nutri-Score was robust for 56 products (56%), while 44
remained FVL-dependent.
On the 56 robust cases, the continuous NutriGuard score
correlated with inverse Nutri-Score class at
ρ= 0.687, p= 4.86×10 −9.(16)
After collapsing NutriGuard into five classes, exact agree-
ment was 28.6% and Cohen’sκ= 0.193.
This is an alignment/contrast analysis rather than clinical
validation because the two systems encode different constructs.
VI. DISCUSSION
Four observations follow.
First, numerical behavior is dominated by nutrition and
NOV A processing: nutrition has the largest direct ablation
effect, while NOV A produces a large multiplicative influence
and perturbation swing.
Second, age adaptation is measurable but moderate for most
products, with a mean adult–child difference of 4.31 points.
Third, coefficient sensitivity distinguishes ranking stability
from grade stability: relative ordering is robust under the tested
±50%perturbations, whereas discrete grade thresholds are
more fragile near boundaries.
Fourth, the architecture creates an auditable boundary be-
tween generative and deterministic computation, so identical
structured inputs and configurations produce identical numer-
ical scores regardless of explanation wording.
The Nutri-Score comparison further shows that NutriGuard
is not simply reproducing an established external score. The
moderate correlation and low categorical agreement indicate
partial alignment while also reflecting different constructs and
algorithmic design choices.
Therefore, the result should be interpreted as a reference-
system comparison rather than evidence of superiority.
VII. VALIDATIONSCOPE, LIMITATIONS,ANDFUTURE
WORK
The current evidence supports software correctness, deter-
minism, component sensitivity, parameter robustness, descrip-
tive NOV A alignment, and implementation-level data-quality
handling.
It does not establish external nutritional validity, clinical
validity, health outcomes, or population-level effects.
Several limitations constrain interpretation.
1) The evaluation contains only 100 product records and is
not representative of the Indian packaged-food market.
2) Age-specific references, coefficients, thresholds, multi-
pliers, and penalties are engineering parameters rather
than clinically calibrated quantities.
3) NOV A is a high-leverage dependency, and the dataset
is highly imbalanced, limiting interpretation of NOV A
correlations.

4) Upstream label-extraction errors may propagate into the

5) Explanation quality has not been evaluated with stan-

6) Internal B1–B4 comparisons are not external published

7) Counterfactuals measure changes in the model’s own
score rather than real-world health outcomes.
Future work should establish a larger, independently curated
and stratified Indian packaged-food benchmark with verified
label images and structured ground truth.
Extraction should be evaluated using field-level precision,
recall, and F1. Independently annotated and more balanced
NOV A labels should also be obtained.
Further work should assess explanation faithfulness with do-
main experts and compare against established external scoring
systems such as Nutri-Score and FSSAI-aligned indices using
matched inputs.
Parameters should be calibrated using transparent expert- or
outcome-based procedures, and uncertainty estimation should
be added for incomplete or ambiguous extraction.
Versioning the dataset, scoring configuration, ingredient
rules, additive mappings, and evaluation scripts together would
further improve reproducibility.
VIII. CONCLUSION
NutriGuard-AI presents a transparent, age-aware, deter-
ministic framework for packaged-food label assessment that
integrates nutrient composition, ingredient characteristics,
food processing, additive exposure, data-quality safeguards,
evidence-oriented explanation, and counterfactual analysis
while strictly separating generative AI from numerical decision
computation.
Evaluation on 100 packaged-food records demonstrated
bounded and deterministic execution, strong sensitivity to
nutrition and NOV A components, measurable age-dependent
score changes, stable product ranking under±50%coefficient
perturbation, descriptive alignment with recorded NOV A la-
bels, and an interpretable counterfactual mechanism.
The Nutri-Score comparison provides an external reference
point without being treated as validation.
Collectively, these findings support reproducibility, in-
spectability, and internal consistency of the implemented
framework, but they do not establish clinical validity,
population-level health effects, or superiority over external
nutrition-scoring systems.
NutriGuard-AI is therefore best positioned as a research
prototype for transparent intelligent information processing
of packaged-food labels, with a clear path toward stronger
external, expert, and clinical validation.
