ABSTRACT
Electric vehicle battery management systems require fast access to internal cell states such  
as  lithium  concentration,  temperature,  and  diffusion-induced  stress,  while  conventional  
numerical  approaches  based  on  discretisation  and  time  stepping  are  computationally  
expensive for real-time use. This project develops a multi-physics Physics-Informed Neural  
Network (PINN) framework that embeds governing physical laws directly into neural-
network training.
The Phase I implementation uses the PyBaMM SPMe model with lumped thermal coupling  
to  generate  a  ground-truth  dataset  over  twenty  constant-current  discharge  conditions  
spanning 0.5C to 2.0C and ambient temperatures from 10°C to 35°C. The dataset contains  
43,200 labelled spatio-temporal points. Diffusion-induced radial and tangential stresses are  
obtained through analytical post-processing using a spherical-particle formulation because  
the selected PyBaMM workflow does not directly provide these stress fields.
A single feed-forward network with four inputs and four outputs is trained using Fickian  
diffusion, a lumped thermal energy balance, mechanical equilibrium, boundary constraints,  
and sparse labelled data. The model contains 67,210 trainable parameters and uses hard-
constrained output forms for the initial concentration, initial temperature, and free-surface  
radial  stress.  Training  combines  Adam  and  L-BFGS,  followed  by  residual-adaptive  
refinement when the held-out concentration error exceeds the target.
On the  held-out  test  split, the  final  temperature  relative  L2 error  is 0.03%  with R²  =  
0.99938, while concentration, radial stress, and tangential stress achieve relative L2 errors  
of 28.50%, 18.90%, and 25.10%, respectively. The PINN inference time is approximately  
0.71 ms for 2,160 field points on a Tesla T4, compared with 464 ms for a PyBaMM SPMe  
discharge, giving a measured speed-up of about 657×. The remaining accuracy gap is traced 
to imbalance in adaptive loss weighting, particularly under-enforcement of the surface flux  
and labelled-data terms.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 GENERAL
Lithium-ion batteries are central to modern electric vehicles, and the battery management  
system  (BMS)  must  maintain  safe  and  efficient  operation  while  responding  rapidly  to 
changing operating conditions. Terminal voltage and current are readily measurable, but  
important  internal  quantities  such  as  lithium  concentration  gradients,  cell  temperature  
distribution,  and  mechanical  stress  are  not  directly  available  from  conventional  BMS  
measurements.  These  internal  states  influence  power  capability,  thermal  safety,  
degradation, and mechanical failure.
Conventional  high-fidelity  battery  simulation  uses  numerical  solvers  such  as  finite-
difference or finite-element methods. These approaches discretise the governing equations  
over a mesh and advance the solution through time. Their computational cost increases with  
spatial resolution, simulation duration, and the number of coupled physical domains. This  
makes direct deployment of a full multi-physics solver inside a vehicle control loop difficult 
when the controller has only milliseconds of computation time.
Physics-Informed  Neural  Networks  provide  an  alternative  by  representing  the  solution  
fields with a differentiable neural network and incorporating the governing equations into  
the  training loss. Automatic  differentiation supplies the  required derivatives, while  the  
network  learns  a  continuous  approximation  of  the  physical  fields.  After  training,  the  
solution can be evaluated with a single forward pass rather than a mesh-based time-stepping 
procedure.
1.2 NEED FOR THE STUDY
A useful battery surrogate for an intelligent BMS must balance three requirements: physical  
consistency, prediction speed, and generalisation beyond a small set of operating points.  
Purely data-driven networks can be fast after training, but their predictions depend heavily  
on the coverage and quality of labelled cycling data. Numerical solvers provide physically  
meaningful reference solutions but are too expensive for repeated real-time inference. A  
PINN can combine sparse reference data with the physical equations, creating a model that  
is both computationally compact and constrained by known battery behaviour.
9

The present work focuses on coupling electrochemical transport, thermal behaviour, and  
diffusion-induced mechanical stress in one cell-level PINN. The project is intentionally  
scoped  to  a  tractable  SPMe  formulation,  constant-current  discharge,  one-way  thermal  
coupling, and analytically derived stress. These boundaries allow the Phase I pipeline to be  
implemented and evaluated systematically while leaving clear extension paths toward a full  
DFN formulation and experimental validation.
1.3 PROBLEM STATEMENT
The problem addressed in this project is the development of a computationally efficient  
neural surrogate capable of predicting internal electrochemical, thermal, and mechanical  
battery states while respecting their governing equations. The model should accept space-
time coordinates and operating-condition descriptors, provide multiple physical fields in  
one forward pass, and achieve sufficiently low latency for future BMS integration. At the  
same time, the evaluation must distinguish genuine accuracy from apparent performance  
caused by data leakage or an inadequate test split.
1.4 OBJECTIVES
1.  Develop  a  single  multi-output  PINN  representing  electrochemical,  thermal,  and  
mechanical behaviour of a lithium-ion cell.
2. Embed Fickian diffusion, a lumped thermal energy balance, and mechanical equilibrium  
directly into the physics-informed objective.
3.  Generate  a  reproducible  PyBaMM  ground-truth  dataset  across  multiple  C-rates  and  
ambient temperatures, with train, validation, and test conditions separated by operating  
condition.
4. Validate the PINN against the numerical reference using relative L2 error, RMSE, MAE,  
MAPE, R², maximum absolute error, and inference latency.
5. Establish a deployment-oriented inference module and identify the remaining accuracy  
bottleneck for the next project phase.
10

1.5 SCOPE AND CONTRIBUTIONS
The main Phase I contribution is an end-to-end pipeline that connects numerical ground-
truth generation, analytical stress post-processing, data scaling, collocation sampling, PINN  
construction, physics-informed training, residual-adaptive fine-tuning, full-grid evaluation,  
and deployment export. The implementation uses one network with four outputs: solid-
phase concentration c(r,t), cell temperature T(t), radial stress σr, and tangential stress σθ.
The current implementation demonstrates that the thermal field can be learned with very  
high  accuracy  and  that  the  trained  network  can  provide  a  substantial  inference-speed  
advantage  over  the  reference  SPMe  solve.  It  also  produces  a  useful  negative  result:  
additional  collocation  points  alone  do  not  remove  the  concentration  bias.  The  failure  
analysis identifies adaptive loss weighting as the likely cause, which provides a focused  
direction for the next phase.

CHAPTER 2
REVIEW OF LITERATURE
2.1 INTRODUCTION
The literature reviewed for this project spans foundational PINN methodology, physics-
informed battery state  estimation, degradation modelling, and early attempts to couple  
multiple battery physics domains. The review indicates that PINNs are increasingly used as  
surrogates or state estimators, but the coupling of electrochemical, thermal, and mechanical  
behaviour in one cell-level model remains comparatively underexplored.
The project therefore treats the literature review not only as a survey of algorithms, but also  
as a design constraint. The selected architecture, loss formulation, data split, and evaluation  
strategy are motivated by limitations observed in earlier studies.
2.2 FOUNDATIONS OF PINNs
Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis introduced the widely adopted PINN framework in  
which governing differential equations are incorporated into a neural-network objective  
through automatic differentiation [1]. Karniadakis and co-authors subsequently positioned  
physics-informed  machine  learning  as  a  broader  family  of  approaches  that  combines  
physical priors with data and neural architectures [2]. These works establish the central idea  
used here: the neural network is not trained only to match observations; it is also penalised  
when its derivatives violate the governing equations.
For battery applications, this principle is attractive because high-fidelity internal states are  
difficult to measure densely. A sparse numerical reference solution can anchor the model,  
while the PDE residual supplies additional structure between labelled points. However, the  
balance among data, boundary conditions, and different physics residuals becomes a critical  
optimisation issue when several fields have very different scales.
12

2.3 PINNs FOR BATTERY MODELLING
Nascimento et al. developed a hybrid Bayesian PINN framework for lithium-ion battery  
prognosis, showing how governing equations can be combined with neural models and  
uncertainty estimation [3]. Hofmann et al. used P2D battery simulations together with  
laboratory  and  field  measurements  for  state-of-health  estimation  [4].  Wang  et  al.  
incorporated physics and monotonicity constraints into degradation modelling and reported  
strong prognosis performance across hundreds of cells [5].
Other work has focused on state estimation and reduced-order battery models. Singh et al.  
incorporated the Fick diffusion equation from a single-particle formulation into a PINN for  
battery state estimation [7]. Hassanaly et al. developed multi-fidelity PINN surrogates for  
SPM  and  P2D  models  and  demonstrated  large  computational  speed-ups  for  parameter  
inference [8]. Mendez-Corbacho et al. showed that the single-particle model can be solved  
without labelled data using a physics-informed formulation [9]. These studies motivate the  
use of reduced-order electrochemical physics in the present Phase I implementation while  
also highlighting the gap between single-physics surrogates and fully coupled multi-physics  
models.
2.4 MULTI-PHYSICS BATTERY PINNS
Kim et al. investigated a multiphysics-informed neural network for lithium-ion battery  
thermal runaway prediction by jointly incorporating electrochemical and thermal behaviour  
[6].  This  demonstrates  the  value  of  coupling  more  than  one  physical  mechanism,  but  
mechanical stress is not represented. The project reviewed this as an important limitation  
because swelling and diffusion-induced stress are directly connected to particle integrity  
and long-term degradation.
The project also builds on the PyBaMM modelling ecosystem. Sulzer et al. introduced  
PyBaMM as an open-source framework for mathematical battery modelling [10]. PyBaMM  
is used here as the numerical reference generator rather than as a run-time dependency of  
the exported PINN. Mechanical stress is derived analytically from the concentration field  
using the spherical-particle formulation of Cheng and Verbrugge [11], since the selected  
PyBaMM workflow does not directly provide the required stress field.
DeepXDE  and  related  physics-informed  numerical  libraries  provide  established  
implementation patterns for differentiable differential-equation solving and residual-based  
13

sampling  [12].  The  present  notebook  follows  the  same  general  philosophy  while  
implementing the model directly in PyTorch so that the final inference package can remain  
lightweight.

2.5 RESEARCH GAP
Based on the reviewed sources and the comparison presented in the project presentation,  
existing battery PINN studies primarily address a single physical domain or, in a smaller  
number  of  cases,  a  coupled  electrochemical-thermal  problem.  The  project  identifies  a  
specific  gap  at  the  intersection  of  electrochemical  transport,  thermal  response,  and  
mechanical stress within a single cell-level PINN intended for rapid inference.
The gap is therefore operational as well as scientific. A model that only predicts voltage or  
a single reduced-order state does not expose the internal quantities that a future physics-
aware BMS could use for thermal and mechanical risk assessment. The present project  
addresses this gap by predicting four physical outputs simultaneously and by embedding the 
governing relationships into one composite objective.

CHAPTER 3
METHODOLOGY AND SYSTEM DESIGN
3.1 SYSTEM OVERVIEW
The proposed workflow consists of five implemented modules: (M1) ground-truth data  
generation, (M2) domain scaling and collocation sampling, (M3) PINN construction and  
physics  loss,  (M4)  two-stage  training  with  residual-adaptive  fine-tuning,  and  (M5)  
evaluation and deployment export. The design deliberately separates the numerical solver  
from the final inference path. PyBaMM is used to generate and validate training data, while  
the exported PINN can operate without PyBaMM at run time.
Figure 1. System architecture and data flow.
3.2 GROUND-TRUTH SIMULATION
Ground truth is generated with the Chen2020 parameter set corresponding to an LG M50  
cylindrical cell with nominal capacity of 5 Ah. Each operating condition is simulated as a  
constant-current discharge using the PyBaMM SPMe model with lumped thermal coupling  
and a 2.5 V voltage cut-off. The radial coordinate is sampled at 24 points and time at 90  
points, giving 2,160 spatio-temporal nodes per condition.
For  every  simulation  the  pipeline  stores  radial  lithium  concentration,  cell  temperature, 
temperature rise, terminal voltage, volumetric heat generation, and analytically derived  
radial and tangential stresses. A sanity filter rejects simulations that terminate implausibly  
early relative to the expected discharge duration. This prevents an invalid short discharge  
from entering the training dataset.

3.3 GOVERNING EQUATIONS
Three physical domains are embedded in the loss. The electrochemical domain describes  
solid-phase lithium diffusion inside a spherical active-material particle. The thermal domain  
describes the cell temperature response to a time-dependent volumetric heat source. The  
mechanical domain imposes radial equilibrium between radial and tangential stresses.
Electrochemical diffusion (Fick's second law):
∂c/∂t = Ds [ ∂²c/∂r² + (2/r)(∂c/∂r) ]
At r = 0:  ∂c/∂r = 0      |      At r = Rp:  −Ds ∂c/∂r = jn/F
Thermal energy balance:
ρcp (dT/dt) = q̇ gen(t) − (hA/V)(T − Tamb)
Mechanical equilibrium:
∂σr/∂r + (2/r)(σr − σθ) = 0      |      σr(Rp) = 0
The mechanical stress field is obtained analytically from the concentration field using the  
Cheng–Verbrugge  spherical-particle  formulation.  For  a  radial  position  r,  the  
implementation evaluates volume-averaged concentration terms and then computes σr and  
σθ. This makes stress a genuine additional prediction target rather than a direct PyBaMM  
output.

3.4 NON-DIMENSIONALISATION
PINN optimisation is highly sensitive to scale differences between variables and residuals.  
In this project the raw quantities span approximately 10⁴ mol/m³ for concentration, 300 K  
for temperature, and 10⁷ Pa for stress. Without scaling, the stress derivatives can dominate  
the composite objective. Each field is therefore mapped to an order-one representation.
r)  = r/Rp,   t)  = t/tend,   c)  = c/cmax,   T)  = (T − Tamb)/50 K,   σ) = σ/20 MPa
The network inputs are further mapped to [−1,+1]. The dimensionless diffusion coefficient  
is αk = Ds tend,k / Rp², with values from 1.66 to 6.95 over the operating conditions. The  
dimensionless  surface-flux  constant  ranges  from  −0.1653  to  −0.0413.  The  heat-source  
reconstruction was verified with 0.000% reported error, and the energy balance closes to  
five significant figures.
3.5 PINN ARCHITECTURE
The PINN uses a single feed-forward trunk with four inputs: normalised radius, normalised  
time, C-rate, and ambient temperature. Five hidden layers contain 128 tanh units each,  
followed by four outputs corresponding to concentration, temperature, radial stress, and  
tangential stress. Xavier-normal initialisation is used for the linear layers.

3.6 HARD-CONSTRAINED OUTPUTS AND PHYSICS-INFORMED LOSS
Three conditions are encoded directly into the output algebra rather than being left entirely  
to penalty terms. The concentration output is written as c]  = c] 0,k + t]  Nc, guaranteeing the  
initial concentration. Temperature is written as T = Tamb,k + 50K· t]  NT, guaranteeing the  
initial temperature. Radial stress is written as σr = 20MPa·(1− r] ) Ns, guaranteeing zero  
radial stress at the free surface.
The  remaining  loss  components  are  the  Fick  diffusion  residual,  symmetry  boundary  
residual, surface-flux boundary residual, thermal energy residual, mechanical equilibrium  
residual, and a sparse labelled-data term. Their effective weights are learned using log-
variance parameters:
L = Σi [ exp(−si) Li + si ]
This adaptive weighting removes the need for manual grid search over λ values, but the  
later results show that the same mechanism can become a failure mode when a nearly exact  
residual receives an excessively large effective weight.

3.8 TRAINING STRATEGY
Training uses two stages. Stage 1 applies Adam with cosine-annealed learning rate and  
gradient clipping for 6,000 epochs. Stage 2 switches to L-BFGS with strong Wolfe line  
search; the learned loss weights are frozen so that the quasi-Newton stage sees a stationary  
objective.  The  recorded  run  completed  Adam  in  373.8  s  and  L-BFGS  in  88.0  s,  for  
approximately 462 s of main training.

3.9 RESIDUAL-ADAPTIVE FINE-TUNING
When  held-out  concentration  accuracy  misses  the  2%  relative-L2  target,  the  notebook  
automatically performs residual-adaptive refinement. A large candidate pool is evaluated  
using the Fick residual, the highest-residual points are selected, and 600 new collocation  
points are added per round. Each round performs 1,200 Adam steps followed by L-BFGS  
refinement.
The baseline concentration test error was 33.30%. Three refinement rounds reduced the best 
test error to 28.50%. Because the improvement flattened, the experiment provides evidence  
that  sampling  density  is  not  the  main  limiting  factor.  The  best  checkpoint  is  restored 
automatically after the ladder.
Figure 5. Residual-adaptive fine-tuning across three refinement rounds.
3.10 EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Evaluation  is  performed  on  the  full  24  ×  90  spatio-temporal  grid  for  every  operating 
condition,  rather  than  only  on  the  sampled  collocation  points.  The  dataset  split  is  by  
operating condition to prevent neighbouring points from the same simulation appearing in  
both training and test sets. The test conditions are 1.25C at 20°C and 1.25C at 30°C, which  
are unseen C-rate and ambient-temperature combinations relative to the training set.
The  reported  metrics  are  relative  L2  error,  RMSE,  MAE,  MAPE,  R²,  and  maximum  
absolute error. In addition, inference latency is measured for one 2,160-point field query  
and compared with a full PyBaMM SPMe discharge solve.

CHAPTER 4
IMPLEMENTATION, RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1 IMPLEMENTATION ENVIRONMENT
The notebook is designed to run in Google Colab and is device-agnostic. The recorded  
environment used PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128, PyBaMM 26.8.0.0, NumPy 2.1.3, and a CUDA-
enabled NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU with 15.6 GB memory. A fixed seed of 42 is used for  
reproducibility.  The  full  pipeline,  including  data  generation,  training,  evaluation,  and  
export, is designed to remain within free-tier execution limits.
The  implementation  uses  Python,  PyTorch  for  the  neural  network  and  automatic

4.7 PHYSICS RESIDUALS AND ROOT-CAUSE ANALYSIS
The residual maps provide the key diagnostic result. The Fick residual is low and smooth  
over most of the interior, and mechanical equilibrium is also largely satisfied away from the  
r=0 coordinate singularity. Therefore, the concentration failure cannot be explained simply  
by an inability to learn the PDE.
Figure 9. Spatial-temporal maps of the Fick and mechanical-equilibrium residuals.
The more important evidence is the imbalance between the boundary and data terms. The  
symmetry boundary residual falls to 3.44×10 ⁸, while the surface-flux residual remains at⁻  
2.79×10 ³  and  the  labelled-data  term  remains  at  2.01×10 ¹.  Under  inverse-loss-style⁻ ⁻  
adaptive weighting, the already-satisfied symmetry term can attract an extremely large  
25

effective weight, approximately 3×10⁷ according to the project analysis, while the data term  
receives a weight near 5. This starves the terms that control the absolute concentration  
level.
The  concentration  error  is  almost  identical  on  train,  validation,  and  test  splits  
(approximately 28.4%, 28.4%, and 28.5% after refinement). This is consistent with under-
fitting or loss imbalance rather than over-fitting. The systematic concentration bias then  
propagates to the stress fields because the stress calculation depends on the concentration  
gradient.

4.13 DISCUSSION
Three conclusions follow from the implementation results. First, the proposed architecture  
is  expressive  enough  to  learn  the  thermal  response  with  very  high  accuracy  and  to  
reproduce the qualitative structure of the electrochemical and mechanical fields. Second,  
the  test  split  design  is  strong  enough  to  expose  generalisation  to  unseen  operating  
conditions  rather  than  merely  memorising  neighbouring  points.  Third,  the  dominant  
concentration error is systematic and closely associated with the optimisation objective, not  
simply with inadequate sampling.
This diagnosis is important for the next iteration. Changing the architecture or adding large  
amounts  of  data  would  increase  computational  effort  without  directly  addressing  the  
observed imbalance. The more targeted approach is to normalise the surface-flux residual,  
split the labelled-data loss by output field, and constrain the adaptive-weight range so that  
already-satisfied hard constraints cannot dominate the optimisation.

CHAPTER 5
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
5.1 CONCLUSION
This Phase  I project  developed and evaluated a  Physics-Informed Neural  Network for  
multi-physics simulation of an electric-vehicle lithium-ion battery cell. The final pipeline  
generates reference data with PyBaMM, derives diffusion-induced stress analytically, scales 
the physical variables, samples collocation points, trains a four-output PINN, performs  
residual-adaptive refinement, evaluates the complete spatio-temporal grid, and exports a  
lightweight inference module.
The implementation successfully embeds three governing physical relationships—solid-
phase lithium diffusion, lumped thermal energy balance, and mechanical equilibrium—into  
a single model. Hard constraints guarantee the initial concentration, initial temperature, and  
free-surface radial stress. The dataset contains 43,200 labelled points across 20 operating  
conditions, and the model contains 67,210 trainable parameters.
The results demonstrate a strong computational advantage. A 2,160-point PINN forward  
pass takes about 0.71 ms on a Tesla T4, compared with 464 ms for a PyBaMM SPMe  
discharge, yielding a measured speed-up of approximately 657×. Thermal prediction is also  
highly accurate, with 0.03% relative L2 error and R² = 0.99938 on the held-out test set.
However, the project does not claim that all physical fields are accurate. Concentration  
remains biased low with 28.50% relative L2 error, while radial and tangential stress errors  
are 18.90% and 25.10%. The analysis indicates that adaptive log-variance weighting over-
emphasises already-satisfied terms such as the symmetry boundary condition and under-
emphasises the surface-flux and labelled-data terms. This produces a solution that satisfies  
the interior PDE while remaining offset from the reference concentration level.

5.3 FUTURE WORK
The Phase II Project work is focused on the loss function rather than on a new architecture.  
Three changes are planned:
1.  Normalise  the  surface-flux  residual  by  the  magnitude  of  the  target  flux  so  that  its 
contribution remains comparable across C-rates.
2.  Split the labelled-data term into separate concentration, temperature, radial-stress, and  
tangential-stress components so that each output can adapt its own weighting.
3.  Floor  and  cap  the  adaptive  weights,  excluding  already  hard-satisfied  terms  from  
dominating the objective and limiting the ratio between component weights.
Beyond the loss-function correction, the project plans to extend the electrochemical model  
from  SPMe  toward  the  full  Doyle–Fuller–Newman  (DFN)  formulation,  investigate  
charging  and  realistic  drive-cycle  inputs,  introduce  ageing  and  degradation  states,  and  
validate  the  stress  field  experimentally.  The  Phase  II  BMS  layer  will  also  explore  
SOC/SOH estimation, quantisation, and execution on embedded hardware.
