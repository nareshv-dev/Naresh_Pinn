A Physics-Consistent Electrochemical-- Thermal
PINN with Concentration-Coupled Particle Stress
for Lithium-Ion Battery Surrogate Modeling
1st Ms. Sasikala C, 2 nd Naresh V, 3 rd Mohamed Shaheel A, 4 th Kishore J, 5 th Kishore P . S.
Department of Computer Science and Engineering
KPR Institute of Engineering and T echnology
Coimbatore, T amil Nadu, India
Abstract—Battery surrogates are useful only when
their coupled outputs describe the same physical
state. A network may fit concentration, tempera-
ture, and stress labels separately while violating the
concentration–stress relation inside an electrode par-
ticle. This work examines that specific failure mode
for constant-current discharge. The proposed physics-
consistent multiphysics PINN (PC-MP-PINN) learns
negative-particle lithium concentration and a volume-
averaged cell temperature as functions of position,
time, C-rate, and ambient temperature. Its objective
includes solid-diffusion, symmetry , surface-flux, and
thermal-energy residuals. Particle stresses are then cal-
culated from the predicted concentration using a spher-
ical linear-elastic operator; they are not independent
neural outputs. Reference fields come from PyBaMM
SPMe simulations with the Chen2020 parameter set.
Evaluation is organized by complete operating con-
ditions and compares three model variants at three
label budgets and three random seeds. The reported
measures examine prediction error, conservation, peak
stress, agreement between electrochemical and me-
chanical fields, and inference time. The study concerns
a discharge surrogate built from simulated data. It does
not claim experimental stress validation, cycle aging,
state-of-health estimation, or remaining-life prediction.

I. Introduction
An electric-vehicle battery-management system mea-
sures current, terminal voltage, and a limited number of
temperatures. It cannot directly observe the lithium profile
within an active-material particle or the stress caused by
that profile. Electrochemical models can estimate these
internal variables, although repeated numerical solves be-
come costly when a controller, optimizer, or uncertainty
study needs many queries. The Doyle–F uller–Newman
model gives a detailed porous-electrode description [1].
Reduced formulations, including the single-particle model
with electrolyte dynamics (SPMe), trade some spatial
detail for lower computational cost [2]–[4]. A physics-
informed neural network offers a different approximation:
it fits simulated states while its derivatives are checked
against selected governing equations [5].
Published battery PINNs already address parameter
recovery , electrochemical surrogates, temperature predic-
tion, and health estimation [6]–[9]. Electrochemical, ther-
mal, and mechanical terms in one model are therefore
not suﬀicient evidence of novelty . W eiet al. , for example,
combine those domains with aging mechanisms for state-
of-health estimation [10]. The present study asks a smaller
question that can be tested with the available discharge
data: does a stress field become more credible when the
architecture forces it to come from the predicted concen-
tration?
The earlier project model treated radial and tangen-
tial stress as trainable outputs. A mechanical residual
restricted their shapes but did not make them unique func-
tions of the network’s concentration field. Consequently ,
a fitted stress curve could be acceptable on its own and
still be inconsistent with the electrochemical prediction.
In the revised model, the network predicts concentration
and temperature only . The Cheng–V erbrugge spherical-
particle relation converts concentration to stress [11]. This
removes one source of cross-field disagreement rather than
adding another output head.
The study makes four testable contributions:
1) a discharge PINN parameterized by C-rate and am-
bient temperature, with separate concentration and
lumped-temperature branches;
2) an analytical concentration-to-stress layer that elim-
inates independently fitted mechanical outputs;
3) condition-level data partitions and validation-based
checkpoint selection, so rows from one simulated tra-
jectory cannot appear in both training and testing;
and
4) a matched comparison of three model variants over
three label budgets and three seeds, assessed with
prediction, conservation, coupling, and timing mea-
sures.
The claims remain limited to single-discharge behavior.
Cycle-dependent degradation requires different targets
and a different dataset.

II. Related W ork and Research Gap
A. Electrochemical surrogate models
The original PINN formulation joins observations with
differential-equation residuals during training [5]. Hass-
analy et al. adapted this idea to single-particle and pseudo-
two-dimensional battery models and studied parameter in-
ference at multiple fidelity levels [6], [7]. Their results also
show why conservation terms and staged training matter
when labeled states are sparse. J. W ang et al. used paral-
lel physics-informed branches to estimate electrochemical
parameters across operating regimes [9]. Zhuang et al.
compared conventional PINNs with a F ourier-enhanced
operator model for SPM responses under changing current
[12]. These papers are the closest electrochemical refer-
ences, but none evaluates a particle-stress field constructed
from its own predicted concentration.
B. Recent battery applications
Physics enters battery-learning systems in several ways.
F u et al. placed an equivalent-circuit relation inside a
recurrent spacecraft-battery model, whereas Dang et al.
used a differential-equation constraint for state-of-charge
estimation [13], [14]. Jiang et al. combined fidelity levels
for spatial pack-temperature prediction [16]. Other recent
studies focus on state of health and use transfer learning,
physically chosen features, limited-sample aging data, or
reaction and mechanics priors [15], [17]–[20]. Because these
implementations use the word “physics-informed” for dif-
ferent mechanisms, T able I records both the task and the
point at which physics enters the model.
C. Thermal and mechanical coupling
Y. W anget al. connected electrical and thermal relations
to a recurrent predictor for cell temperature over life-
cycle data [8]. Lyu et al. used electrochemical, thermal,
and particle-mechanics components in a battery digital
twin [21]. Thus, the three-domain combination predates
this paper. The distinction studied here is architectural:
stress is recomputed from concentration instead of learned
in parallel. The required elastic relation follows spheri-
cal insertion-particle analyses [11], [22]. Lithium insertion
changes particle volume, and a nonuniform concentration
then produces different radial and circumferential stresses
[23]. A uniform profile produces no diffusion-induced stress
in this model.
D. Reason for retaining mechanics
Mechanical response and degradation describe different
time scales. The stress considered here is an instantaneous
response to a concentration gradient during a discharge.
Aging models track irreversible change across cycles and
need variables such as capacity , cycle count, SEI thickness,
plated lithium, crack growth, or loss of active material.
Those mechanisms can later be coupled to stress, but the
present simulations do not contain the labels needed to
validate them. Replacing the mechanical calculation with a
degradation equation would therefore change the research
problem without supplying the required evidence. T able I
states this scope explicitly .

V . Experimental Methodology
A. Reference data and condition split
The reference set contains constant-current SPMe dis-
charges with lumped thermal coupling and Chen2020
parameters. A run stops at 2.5 V. W e retain 100 time
samples and 25 radial samples from each run. T raining
uses the Cartesian product of C-rates {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}
and ambient temperatures {10, 25, 35}◦C. V alidation uses
C-rates {0.75, 1.75} at {15, 30}◦C. The two final test cases
are 1.25C discharges at 20◦C and 32.5◦C. In total, the
dataset has 18 trajectories and 45,000 radius–time records.
No trajectory is divided across subsets. V alidation data
determine early stopping and checkpoint choice; the test
trajectories are read only after fitting has ended. Figure 2
shows the sampled operating envelope and the matched
ablation design.
B. Models and optimization settings
Three formulations are trained. The data-only network
reproduces the earlier four-output design and fits concen-
tration, temperature, radial stress, and tangential stress.
The prior-style PINN keeps those outputs and adds
diffusion, thermal, boundary , and mechanical-equilibrium
penalties. The proposed PC-MP-PINN removes both
stress heads and generates stress from its concentration
field.
F or every formulation, each training trajectory con-
tributes either 50, 150, or 300 labeled records. Seeds 11, 22,
and 33 give 27 primary runs. Adam starts at 10−3; gradi-
ents are clipped at norm 10. T raining ends at 2500 epochs
or after 350 epochs without validation improvement. Each

C. Controlled comparisons
The first comparison, data-only versus prior-style PINN,
tests whether the added residuals help the existing four-
output network. The second comparison changes two
linked design choices: it replaces trainable stress outputs
with the analytical operator and removes radius from
the temperature branch. Results from this pair therefore
measure the revised architecture as a whole. They cannot
attribute every change to mechanics alone. Repeating both
comparisons at 50, 150, and 300 labels reveals how their
behavior changes as supervision becomes less sparse.
If computational time permits, three diagnostic runs
can separate these effects: omit the thermal residual but
keep temperature labels; apply the stress operator to
concentration from the data-only network; and replace
only the stress heads in the prior-style PINN while leaving
its temperature input unchanged. These runs aid diagnosis
but do not replace the predeclared three-model experi-
ment.

VI. Results and Discussion
A. Result-reporting rule
T able III reads values produced by the notebook
summaries paper_metrics_summary.csv and
variation_gap_vs_previous.csv. Placeholder text
is retained in this draft because the complete three-seed
experiment has not yet been inserted. A submission
version must run with quick_mode=False and replace
every macro at the start of the source with the measured
mean and standard deviation. No numerical result is
inferred from an incomplete run.
B. Plots and traceability
The notebook exports plots of error against label count,
surface concentration, temperature rise, and both stress
components. A separate panel shows the mass-balance
residual. Each plot names the held-out condition and seed
aggregation. Scalar scores provide a compact comparison,
while trajectories reveal timing offsets, boundary errors,
and short stress peaks that an average may hide.
All summaries are built from the same condition-level
results file. Before aggregation, the script checks for the
27 expected runs, verifies that condition groups do not
overlap, and confirms three seeds for each reported mean.
An unsuccessful run remains visible as incomplete rather
than being dropped from the denominator.
C. Questions answered by the experiment
The experiment tests whether residual training helps
when labels are scarce and whether the analytical me-
chanics layer improves agreement between concentration
and stress without sacrificing the two learned fields. The
evidence must include all seeds, both test conditions, peak
and field stress errors, and the independent conservation
calculation. The test points lie inside the sampled C-rate
and ambient-temperature range. They say nothing about
charging, arbitrary drive cycles, different chemistries, or
extreme temperatures.
D. Expected physical behavior
During discharge, reaction at the particle surface can
create a radial concentration gradient and hence diffusion-
induced stress. A spatially uniform profile gives zero stress
under Eqs. (7) and (8); faster discharge is expected to

VII. Limitations and Threats to V alidity
Every target in this study is simulated. Agreement with
PyBaMM shows that the surrogate reproduces the selected
SPMe configuration; it does not constitute experimental
validation. The model represents one negative-electrode
particle, one cell-average temperature, fixed material prop-
erties, and constant-current discharge. It omits through-
thickness thermal gradients, spatially varying electrode
stress, plastic deformation, binder behavior, fracture ini-
tiation, and two-way stress–diffusion feedback.
The elastic constants are literature values rather than
measurements from the modeled LG M50 cell. The result-
ing stress should be interpreted as a model-derived indica-
tor. Chen2020 parameters were assembled from multiscale
experiments, but a parameter set remains an input to the
mathematical model rather than proof of every internal
property of a particular cell [25].
Heat-generation histories also come from the SPMe
reference. The neural network interpolates within the se-
lected constant-current conditions and has not been tested
with an arbitrary current waveform. PINN optimization
adds another limitation: data and residual terms can pro-
duce gradients of very different sizes. Repeated seeds and
independent residual calculations expose some unstable
fits but cannot remove error in the governing assumptions.
The study makes no aging claim. PyBaMM can repre-
sent SEI growth, plating, active-material loss, and cracking
[24], but a credible degradation experiment would also
need cycling schedules, health or capacity targets, degra-
dation parameters, and held-out cells or profiles. Stress
may later act as an input to such a model; it is not
interchangeable with degradation.
A. Sensitivity and numerical uncertainty
Equations (7) and (8) scale stress by EΩ/(1−ν). Errors
in E or Ω can therefore dominate the mechanical result
even if concentration is accurate. The planned sensitivity
calculation passes each stored concentration field through
the operator after changing E and Ω separately by ±20%.
It records the change in peak stress without retraining.
Because Ds is fixed during fitting, conclusions apply to
the selected Chen2020 diffusivity .
The radial integral is repeated with 25, 50, and 100
grid points. Peak stress and mass-balance error should
approach stable values as the grid is refined; otherwise the
reported difference may be a quadrature artifact.
B. Failure analysis
The saved histories allow failures to be assigned to
specific stages. High concentration error with a small PDE
residual suggests a boundary , scaling, or initial-condition
problem. Low label error with a large off-grid residual
points to poor physical generalization. If concentration is
accurate but stress is not, the elastic constants or radial
integration should be checked. A nearly constant tem-
perature bias suggests heat capacity or heat generation,
whereas an error that changes with ambient temperature
points toward the cooling term.
Non-finite losses, very early checkpoints, and runs still
improving at epoch 2500 are reported with their seeds
rather than replaced. The held-out cases measure inter-
polation only . Robustness would require additional tests
with noise, parameter shifts, and current profiles. A small
residual confirms agreement with the equations imposed
during training; it cannot establish that those equations
include every relevant process.
VIII. Conclusion
This work revises a battery PINN so that its mechan-
ical field is tied directly to its electrochemical field. The
trainable outputs are particle concentration and lumped
temperature. Radial and tangential stresses follow from
the concentration integral, while diffusion and energy-
balance residuals regularize the learned fields. The eval-
uation keeps complete operating conditions separate and
repeats matched model variants across label budgets and
seeds.
The final claim depends on the full experiment. The
redesign is useful only if it removes concentration–stress
disagreement without materially worsening stress, con-
centration, or temperature error. If consistency improves
but prediction does not, the next step is to improve con-
centration learning or calibrate the mechanical constants
rather than claim success from the architectural identity
alone. Experimental cells, dynamic-current profiles, and
cycling data are needed before extending the conclusions
to degradation or battery health.
