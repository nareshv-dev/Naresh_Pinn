A Physics-Consistent Electrochemical--Thermal
PINN with Concentration-Coupled Particle Stress
for Lithium-Ion Battery Surrogate Modeling
1st Ms. Sasikala C, 2 nd Naresh V, 3 rd Mohamed Shaheel A, 4 th Kishore J, 5 th Kishore P. S.
Department of Computer Science and Engineering
KPR Institute of Engineering and Technology
Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India
Abstract—A structured comparison of ten recent
physics-informed battery studies shows strong progress
in electrochemical surrogates, thermal prediction, pa-
rameter estimation, and degradation-aware health
modeling, while also showing that multiphysics cou-
pling alone is not a defensible novelty claim. This
paper therefore studies a narrower gap: a battery
surrogate can remain physically ambiguous when me-
chanical outputs are learned independently of the
electrochemical state that causes them. W e present a
physics-consistent multiphysics PINN (PC-MP-PINN)
for constant-current discharge of a lithium-ion cell. The
model predicts negative-particle lithium concentration
and lumped cell temperature from radius, time, C-
rate, and ambient temperature. Solid diffusion, center
symmetry , surface flux, and energy balance enter the
training loss; radial and tangential stresses are ob-
tained from predicted concentration through an ana-
lytical spherical-particle elasticity operator instead of
independent stress heads. PyBaMM SPMe simulations
with Chen2020 parameters provide reference trajec-
tories. A condition-wise protocol compares data-only ,
independently learned-stress PINN, and proposed vari-
ants across three label budgets and three seeds. Nine
metrics cover field accuracy , peak stress, mass con-
servation, cross-field consistency , and inference speed.
The scope is discharge-state surrogate modeling; ex-
perimental validation, degradation, state of health,
and remaining useful life are not claimed without the
corresponding data.
Index Terms —physics-informed neural network,
lithium-ion battery , SPMe, thermal model, diffusion-
induced stress, multiphysics surrogate, sparse data
I. Introduction
Lithium-ion battery management requires rapid esti-
mates of quantities that cannot be measured directly,
including concentration gradients inside active-material
particles and the stresses induced by repeated lithium in-
sertion and removal. High-fidelity electrochemical models
describe these states through coupled differential equa-
tions, but their repeated numerical solution can be ex-
pensive for optimization, uncertainty analysis, and em-
bedded control. The Doyle–Fuller–Newman formulation
established a foundational porous-electrode description
of galvanostatic lithium-cell behavior [1]. Reduced-order
electrochemical models such as the single-particle model
with electrolyte dynamics (SPMe) retain key transport
behavior at lower cost [2]–[4]. Physics-informed neural
networks provide another reduction mechanism: a neural
approximation is trained simultaneously from available
state labels and residuals of the governing equations [5].
Battery PINN studies now cover electrochemical pa-
rameter identification, thermal-state prediction, SPM and
pseudo-two-dimensional (P2D) surrogates, and health es-
timation [6]–[9]. Therefore, a claim that electrochemical,
thermal, and mechanical quantities merely appear in one
network is insuﬀicient. In particular, Wei et al. already
introduced an electrochemical–thermal–mechanical aging-
informed framework for state-of-health estimation that in-
cludes solid-electrolyte-interphase growth, lithium plating,
and particle fracture [10]. The scientific question must be
more specific.
The weakness addressed here is cross-field consistency.
In a prior formulation, radial stress and tangential stress
were direct neural outputs. A mechanical-equilibrium
residual constrained those outputs, but it did not uniquely
force them to arise from the predicted lithium concentra-
tion. A network could therefore fit plausible stress labels
while drifting from the electrochemical state. We replace
those stress heads with the Cheng–Verbrugge spherical-
particle stress operator [11]. This creates an explicit causal
chain from concentration to stress.
The contributions are:
1) a parameterized PINN for negative-particle concen-
tration and lumped temperature over C-rate and
ambient-temperature conditions;
2) a deterministic concentration-to-stress construction
that removes independent mechanical degrees of
freedom;
3) leakage-resistant evaluation using condition-wise
train, validation, and test sets, with checkpoint se-
lection based only on validation data;
4) a controlled comparison across three formulations,
three label budgets, and three random seeds using
field, peak, conservation, consistency, and inference-
eﬀiciency metrics; and
5) a precise statement of scope separating single-
discharge mechanics from cycle-dependent degrada-

II. Related Work and Research Gap
A. Physics-Informed Battery Surrogates
Raissi et al. established the modern PINN framework
by combining observation loss with residuals of differen-
tial equations [5]. In battery modeling, Hassanaly et al.
developed SPM and P2D PINN surrogates for parameter
inference, showing both the promise of low-data training
and the importance of conservation regularization and
hierarchical training [6], [7]. Jingrong Wang et al. used
multiple PINN branches to identify electrochemical pa-
rameters across operating conditions [9]. Zhuang et al.
later benchmarked conventional PINNs and a DeepONet-
based architecture for SPM surrogate modeling [12]. These
works motivate electrochemical residuals and careful low-
data evaluation, but particle stress is outside their main
prediction target.
B. Recent 2024--2026 Context
Recent work has broadened physics-informed battery
learning beyond PDE surrogates. Fu et al. embedded
an equivalent-circuit model in a recurrent network for
spacecraft-battery modeling, while Dang et al. imposed
differential-equation structure for state-of-charge estima-
tion [13], [14]. Jiang et al. combined low- and high-
fidelity information for battery-pack heat-map prediction
[16]. Recent health studies use physics-informed features,
transfer learning, small-sample degradation dynamics, and
electrochemical–thermal–mechanical side-reaction priors
[15], [17]–[20]. These studies reinforce the need to state
whether physics enters through equations, architecture,
features, or regularization; the present work specifically
enforces concentration–stress consistency in a discharge
surrogate.
C. Thermal and Mechanical Coupling
Yuchen Wang et al. embedded electrical, heat-
generation, and thermal relations in a recurrent battery-
informed model for life-cycle temperature prediction [8].
Lyu et al. coupled electrochemical, thermal, and particle-
stress models in a digital twin, directly demonstrating that
the presence of all three domains is not itself new [21].
Their objectives differ from the present sparse-label com-
parison of independently predicted versus concentration-
derived stress. The analytical mechanics used here follows
spherical-particle stress models [11], [22]. Insertion-driven
volume change couples stress with transport, reaction, de-
formation, and fracture [23]. Uniform concentration gives
zero diffusion-induced stress; nonuniformity generates ra-
dial and tangential stress.
D. Why Mechanics Is Retained
Replacing mechanics with degradation is not necessary
for the present research question. Mechanics describes
an instantaneous consequence of a concentration gradient
during one discharge. Degradation describes irreversible
evolution over many cycles and requires cycle index, ca-
pacity or state-of-health labels, and mechanisms such as
SEI growth, lithium plating, active-material loss, or crack
propagation. Both can coexist in a future model, but
adding degradation changes the target from a discharge
surrogate to an aging model. The current dataset does
not support that change. Table I positions the proposed
work without claiming that multiphysics coupling itself is
unprecedented.

IV. Proposed Physics-Consistent PINN
A. Architecture and Hard Constraints
Figure 1 shows the model. Inputs are normalized radius
¯r = r/Rp, normalized physical time τ = t/tref, C-rate
C, and ambient temperature Tamb. The fixed scale tref =
7200 s is used for every condition. This avoids normalizing
by each trajectory’s final discharge time, which would
reveal future information at inference.
Two fully connected subnetworks use four hidden layers

concentration head receives (¯r, τ, C, Tamb); the lumped

D. Reproducible Implementation Procedure
Each operating condition is first solved independently
with PyBaMM. Physical time, interfacial current den-
sity, heat generation, volume-averaged temperature, and
negative-particle concentration are exported on a common
time–radius grid. The implementation then follows five
fixed stages:
1) construct the condition-level train, validation, and
test partitions before drawing any labeled rows;
2) fit normalization constants from training conditions
only and retain the physical units needed by the
residuals;
3) draw the requested number of labeled points and
a separate set of interior and boundary collocation
points for each training condition;
4) update network parameters with Adam, save the
state with the lowest validation objective, and stop
after the patience limit; and
5) restore that state once, evaluate every held-out con-
dition, derive stress from the predicted concentra-
tion, and export metrics without further optimiza-
tion.
All three model variants reuse the same condition split
and the same seed-specific labeled indices. This pairing
makes the difference between models less sensitive to
which samples happen to be selected. Collocation points
are redrawn during training, while validation and test
grids remain fixed. The experiment records the software
version, parameter set, seed, label budget, selected epoch,
elapsed training time, and number of trainable parameters
with every checkpoint.
The analytical stress operator requires a radial integral.
Concentration predictions are sorted by radius for each
condition and time, integrated with a cumulative trape-
zoidal rule, and evaluated at the surface. The limiting
value at r = 0 is handled analytically rather than by divid-
ing by r3. This detail is essential: clipping the denominator
without applying the correct limit can create an artificial
center stress and contaminate the peak-stress metric.
V. Experimental Methodology
A. Reference Data and Operating-Condition Split
Constant-current discharge trajectories are generated
using PyBaMM SPMe with lumped thermal coupling
and the Chen2020 parameter set. Each trajectory ter-
minates at 2.5 V and is sampled at 100 times and
25 radial locations. Twelve training conditions com-
bine C-rates {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0} with ambient tempera-
tures {10, 25, 35}◦C. Four validation conditions combine
{0.75, 1.75}C with {15, 30}◦C. Two held-out test condi-
tions use 1.25C at 20◦C and 32.5◦C. This produces 18
conditions and 45,000 radial-time rows.
The split is performed by complete operating condition,
not by randomly mixing rows from the same simulation
across sets. Validation controls checkpoint selection and
early stopping; test errors are computed only after train-
ing. Figure 2 summarizes the envelope and controlled
experiments.

B. Baselines and Training Protocol
Three variants are compared. Data-only uses the
same four-output architecture as the earlier notebook
and minimizes labeled concentration, temperature, and
stress errors. Prior-style PINN adds diffusion, ther-
mal, boundary, and mechanical-equilibrium residuals but
retains independent stress heads. Proposed PC-MP-
PINN predicts only concentration and temperature and
derives stress from concentration.
The full experiment uses label budgets of 50, 150, and
300 samples per training condition and seeds 11, 22,
and 33, giving 27 runs. Adam uses a learning rate of
10−3, gradient norm clipping at 10, at most 2500 epochs,
and patience of 350 epochs. Physics collocation uses 900
interior and 120 boundary samples per training condition.
The selected checkpoint minimizes a validation objective
and is stored before test evaluation.
C. Controlled Ablations
The comparison separates two levels of change. Data-
only versus prior-style PINN measures the effect of adding
the available residuals while retaining the earlier four-
output architecture. Prior-style PINN versus PC-MP-
PINNevaluates a joint redesign: replacement of the inde-
pendent stress heads by the analytical concentration-to-
stress map and removal of radius from the temperature
branch. It cannot by itself assign an accuracy difference to
only one of those changes. The three label budgets measure
sample eﬀiciency, and all budgets are reported.
Three diagnostic ablations strengthen interpretation if
compute permits: remove the thermal residual while re-
taining temperature labels; derive stress from the data-
only model’s concentration; and retain the prior tempera-
ture branch while replacing only its stress heads with the
analytical operator. The last comparison isolates the stress
construction from the temperature-branch change. These
diagnostics remain secondary to the predeclared three-
model comparison.

E. Statistical Summary and Acceptance Criteria
For each model–budget pair, metrics are evaluated sep-
arately for the two test conditions and three seeds. The
primary summary is the seed mean and sample standard
deviation after averaging the two conditions within each
seed. The worst-condition column is retained because an
apparently strong mean can conceal failure at one ambient
temperature. Since only three seeds are used, the standard
deviation is descriptive; it should not be presented as proof
of statistical significance.
A result supports the central hypothesis only when three
conditions hold together: the proposed model reduces
concentration–stress inconsistency as designed, its stress
RMSE or peak-stress error does not materially worsen, and
its concentration and temperature errors remain compet-
itive over the label budgets. A zero consistency residual
alone is a property of the architecture, not evidence of
predictive accuracy. Inference latency is accepted only
when the protocol in Fig. 3 is followed; training time and
parameter count remain secondary resource measures.

C. Questions the Experiment Must Resolve
The evaluation asks whether physics constraints reduce
sparse-label error and whether analytical concentration–
stress coupling improves mechanical credibility without
degrading concentration or temperature accuracy. Evi-
dence requires consistent multi-seed behavior, peak-stress
and stress-RMSE checks, conservation results, and worst-
condition reporting. Held-out conditions test interpolation
inside the sampled C-rate and temperature envelope; they
do not establish extrapolation to charging, drive cycles,
extreme temperatures, or unseen chemistries.
D. Physical Interpretation and Prior Work
During discharge, a surface-to-core concentration gra-
dient produces diffusion-induced stress, while uniform
concentration gives zero stress; higher rates should in-
crease transient nonuniformity. Temperature rise follows
heat generation and convective loss in Eq. (5). Un-
like parameter-inference surrogates [6], [7], aging-informed
SOH models [10], and life-cycle temperature predictors [8],
this study tests analytical mechanical consistency during
discharge. These expectations guide interpretation but do
not replace measured errors.
VII. Limitations and Threats to Validity
All labels are simulated. PyBaMM is a credible and
reproducible reference environment, but agreement with
an SPMe simulation is not experimental validation. The
model uses a negative-electrode representative particle,
lumped cell temperature, prescribed material parameters,
and constant-current discharge. It does not represent
through-cell temperature gradients, heterogeneous elec-
trode stress, plasticity, binder effects, fracture initiation,
or feedback from stress to diffusion.
The mechanical constants are assumed rather than
calibrated to the particular LG M50 cell. Consequently,
stress results are best interpreted as analytical diffusion-
induced stress indicators. The Chen2020 parameter set
was developed from multiscale experiments, yet PyBaMM
documentation cautions that parameter sets are model
inputs rather than certificates of a cell’s true internal prop-
erties [25]. Material sensitivity analysis is recommended
before making quantitative mechanical claims.
The heat-generation history in the training references
is obtained from the SPMe simulation. The resulting
model learns a parameterized mapping over the sampled
constant-current envelope; it has not been shown to accept
arbitrary current profiles. Additionally, PINN optimiza-
tion can suffer from competing gradient scales. Multiple
seeds and independent residual checks reduce the risk of
reporting a favorable but unstable checkpoint, but they
do not eliminate model-form error.
No degradation claim is supported. Adding SEI growth,
lithium plating, loss of active material, or crack propaga-
tion is scientifically possible in PyBaMM [24]; however,
a defensible aging study also requires cycling protocols,
capacity or health targets, degradation parameters, and
held-out cells or profiles. Mechanical state and degradation
can coexist, but one should not be substituted for the other
merely to increase the number of equations.
A. Parameter Sensitivity and Uncertainty
Stress scales with EΩ/(1 − ν) in Eqs. (7) and (8), so
uncertain material constants can dominate stress error
even when concentration is accurate. The sensitivity study
passes each saved concentration prediction through the
analytical operator while varying E and Ω independently
by ±20%; it reports the corresponding peak-stress change
without retraining. Because Ds is fixed during training,
conclusions remain restricted to Chen2020 rather than
changing diffusivity after fitting.
Quadrature uncertainty is checked at 25, 50, and 100
radial points. Peak stress and mass-balance error must
stabilize with refinement, separating numerical integration
error from a genuine physics violation.
B. Failure Analysis Protocol
Failures are separated into data, physics, and coupling
errors. Large concentration error with a small residual
points to boundary, initialization, or scaling error; small
data error with a large residual indicates unreliable off-
grid physics. Accurate concentration with poor stress
implicates material constants or quadrature. A tempera-
ture offset suggests heat-generation or heat-capacity error,
whereas ambient-dependent growth suggests cooling mis-
match.
Non-finite runs, implausibly early checkpoints, and runs
still improving at the epoch limit are flagged from vali-
dation and residual histories; their causes and counts are
reported without replacing diﬀicult seeds. The held-out
conditions test interpolation only. Claims of robustness
require additional noise, parameter shifts, or current-
profile tests, and a low residual establishes agreement with
the imposed equations rather than completeness of the
physics.

VIII. Conclusion
This paper defines an electrochemical–thermal PINN
with particle stress derived analytically from predicted
concentration. It also uses lumped thermal physics,
fixed physical-time normalization, condition-wise splits,
validation-only checkpointing, and controlled sparse-label
evaluation. The contribution is this cross-field construc-
tion and its evaluation protocol, not a claim that
electrochemical–thermal–mechanical modeling is new.
The empirical conclusion must use the complete three-
seed run. Lower stress error and label sensitivity with
retained concentration and temperature accuracy would
support structural physics coupling. If only consistency
improves, the result instead motivates better concen-
tration training, adaptive loss balancing, or calibrated
material parameters. Future work will add experiments,
dynamic currents, uncertainty analysis, and degradation
with appropriate cycling data.
